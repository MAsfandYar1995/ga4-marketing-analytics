# GA4 Marketing Analytics

An end-to-end marketing analytics project using **Google Analytics 4 event data, BigQuery SQL and Power BI** to analyse acquisition performance, ecommerce conversion, user retention and customer behaviour.

The project transforms raw nested GA4 event data into session-level analytical tables, evaluates the quality of acquisition channels, compares new and returning users, measures D7/D30 retention, and explores repeat purchasing behaviour.

## Project Overview

The goal of this project was to move beyond basic website reporting and answer commercially useful questions about the quality of traffic being acquired.

The analysis focuses on four areas:

- Marketing acquisition performance
- Ecommerce funnel behaviour
- User retention and cohort performance
- New, returning and repeat customer behaviour

The final dataset contains approximately:

## Business Questions

The project was designed to answer questions such as:

- Which acquisition channels generate the most traffic?
- Which channels generate the highest conversion rates and revenue per session?
- Where do users drop out of the ecommerce funnel?
- How does behaviour differ between new and returning users?
- Do acquired users return after their first visit?
- Which acquisition channels generate stronger D7 and D30 retention?
- How much revenue is generated by returning users?
- What percentage of purchasers make another purchase?

- `event_name`

## Data Preparation and Modelling

### Session Identification

GA4 data is event-based, so events were first grouped into sessions.

A unique session identifier was created using:

```sql
CONCAT(
    user_pseudo_id,
    '-',
    ga_session_id
)
```

This allowed event-level activity to be aggregated into one row per session.

### Session Acquisition Attribution

Source, medium and campaign values were extracted from GA4 event parameters.

Because traffic information is not populated consistently on every event, session attribution was derived from the earliest relevant traffic information available within each session.

Sessions were then classified into marketing channel groups including:

- Organic Search
- Direct
- Referral
- Paid Search
- Affiliate
- Email
- Self-referral
- Unattributed
- Data Unavailable
- Other

### Ecommerce Session Model

Session-level flags were created for the main ecommerce events:

- View Item
- Add to Cart
- Begin Checkout
- Add Shipping Info
- Add Payment Info
- Purchase

Revenue was also aggregated at session level.

The acquisition and ecommerce tables were then combined into the main analytical session table used by Power BI.

### New vs Returning Users

Sessions were classified using GA4's `ga_session_number`:

- `ga_session_number = 1` -> New
- `ga_session_number > 1` -> Returning

This allowed conversion, revenue and funnel behaviour to be compared between first-time and returning sessions.

### Retention Analysis

The analysis also measures exact-day retention.

For each user's first observed session:

- **D7 retained** means the user returned exactly 7 days after their first session.
- **D30 retained** means the user returned exactly 30 days after their first session.

Users were only included in each retention denominator when sufficient observation time existed in the dataset.

Retention was analysed by:

- Weekly acquisition cohort
- Acquisition channel

### Repeat Purchase Analysis

A purchaser-level table was created from purchasing sessions.

Purchasers were grouped into:

- 1 Purchase
- 2 Purchases
- 3+ Purchases

This was used to calculate the proportion of purchasers who made another purchase during the analysis period.

## Key Findings

### 1. Returning users are considerably more valuable

Returning users converted at **3.10% compared with 0.68% for new users**, approximately **4.6x higher**.

Revenue per session was also substantially higher:

- New users: **$0.47**
- Returning users: **$2.43**

Returning-user revenue per session was therefore approximately **5.2x higher**.

### 2. Returning users generate most revenue despite representing a minority of sessions

Returning users represented approximately **27.5% of sessions**, but generated **66.41% of total revenue**.

This suggests that user value increases substantially once visitors return to the site.

### 3. Repeat purchasing is limited

There were **4,419 purchasers** during the analysis period:

- **4,057** purchased once
- **308** purchased twice
- **54** purchased three or more times

Only **8.19% of purchasers made another purchase**, meaning approximately **91.8% purchased only once**.

### 4. Returning users progress through the funnel more effectively

Among sessions that viewed a product, **12.35% of returning-user sessions reached purchase compared with 3.41% of new-user sessions**.

The difference became particularly noticeable deeper in the purchase journey, including the payment stage.

### 5. Overall exact-day retention is low

Across eligible users:

- **D7 retention: 0.68%**
- **D30 retention: 0.14%**

These metrics represent exact-day retention rather than users returning at any point within 7 or 30 days.

### 6. Acquisition quality varies substantially by channel

Organic Search generated the largest share of traffic, but traffic volume alone did not determine commercial value.

Referral showed strong performance across conversion, revenue per session and retention.

Affiliate recorded the highest D7 retention rate at **2.96%**, but this was based on only **405 eligible users**, so the result should be interpreted cautiously.

## Business Recommendations

### Improve first-time user conversion

Returning users considerably outperform new users throughout the funnel.

The largest opportunity is therefore not simply generating additional traffic, but improving the ability of first-time visitors to progress towards purchase.

Potential areas for investigation include:

- Checkout friction
- Delivery information
- Trust signals
- Guest checkout
- Payment experience
- Product and offer clarity

These should be validated through controlled experimentation rather than assuming the cause from behavioural data alone.

### Increase post-visit and post-purchase re-engagement

Returning users generate a disproportionate share of revenue.

Marketing activity should therefore focus on encouraging first-time visitors and purchasers to return through tactics such as:

- Remarketing
- Email capture
- Lifecycle campaigns
- Personalised product recommendations
- Post-purchase communication

### Increase second-purchase conversion

With only **8.19% of purchasers buying again**, the transition from first purchase to second purchase represents a clear retention opportunity.

Potential strategies include:

- Second-purchase incentives
- Replenishment reminders
- Product recommendations
- Post-purchase email journeys
- Loyalty initiatives

### Investigate high-quality Referral traffic

Referral traffic combines relatively strong conversion and retention performance.

The next step should be to identify the specific referral sources responsible for this performance and determine whether successful partnerships or placements can be expanded.

### Optimise Organic Search for commercial outcomes

Organic Search generates substantial traffic, but traffic volume should not be treated as the only measure of success.

High-traffic organic landing pages should also be assessed using:

- Conversion rate
- Revenue per session
- Funnel progression
- Returning-user behaviour

This can help identify pages that attract traffic but underperform commercially.

### Avoid over-interpreting small channels

Channels with low traffic volumes can produce high headline rates from relatively small samples.

Affiliate, for example, recorded strong D7 retention but had only **405 eligible users**.

Additional observations or controlled tests should be collected before making major budget decisions based on these rates.

## Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

### Short observation period

The dataset covers approximately three months, limiting the ability to evaluate long-term seasonality, customer lifetime behaviour or longer retention windows.

### Exact-day retention

D7 and D30 retention measure whether a user returned exactly 7 or 30 days after their first session.

They do not measure whether a user returned at any time within the first 7 or 30 days.

### Anonymous user identification

GA4 `user_pseudo_id` represents an anonymous browser/device identifier rather than a confirmed customer account.

The same person using multiple devices or browsers may therefore appear as multiple users.

### Repeat purchases are based on purchasing sessions

Repeat-purchaser analysis uses separate sessions containing a purchase as the purchase-frequency measure.

It is therefore a session-based approximation rather than a customer-level order history based on a persistent CRM customer identifier.

### Attribution quality

Some GA4 traffic values are unavailable, incomplete or classified as self-referrals.

Channel comparisons were therefore interpreted with care, and unreliable categories were excluded from some detailed acquisition analyses.

### Partial cohort periods

Incomplete cohort periods at the beginning or end of the dataset were excluded or left blank where appropriate to avoid treating incomplete observation windows as poor retention.

## Project Outcome

The analysis shows that acquisition volume alone does not explain customer value.

Returning users represent a much smaller share of sessions but generate the majority of revenue, convert substantially more effectively and progress further through the ecommerce funnel.

At the same time, both exact-day retention and repeat purchasing remain low.

The main commercial opportunity identified by the analysis is therefore to improve the transition from **first visit to return visit, and from first purchase to repeat purchase**, while continuing to evaluate acquisition channels based on downstream customer quality rather than traffic volume alone.
